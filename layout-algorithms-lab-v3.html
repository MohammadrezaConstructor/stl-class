The six methods in this lab, and how they relate

All of them answer one question — which department goes where? — but split into two families by the data they use and by construction vs improvement.

  • CORELAP (1967) — construction; qualitative (REL chart); deterministic; places the highest-TCR dept first, then grows outward by placement rating.
  • ALDEP (Seehof & Evans, 1967) — construction; qualitative; random first department, minimum-closeness selection, serpentine sweep; run many times, keep the best.
  • CRAFT (Armour & Buffa, 1963) — improvement; quantitative (from–to flows); best pairwise exchange until no swap helps — a hill-climber that halts at a local optimum.
  • Simulated Annealing (1983) — improvement metaheuristic; accepts occasional worse layouts to escape local optima; the direct answer to CRAFT's main weakness.
  • Genetic Algorithm (Holland, 1975) — population metaheuristic; breeds and mutates whole layouts; explores many regions at once.

The quantitative methods (CRAFT, SA, GA) all minimise the same Σ fᵢⱼ·dᵢⱼ — the Quadratic Assignment Problem. Because the number of possible layouts is — for the current department count, and QAP is NP-hard, we rely on heuristics rather than checking every layout.

Theory: the CORELAP procedure
  1. Compute TCRᵢ = Σⱼ v(relᵢⱼ) with A=6…X=1. Highest TCR enters first, at the center.
  2. Next entry: the department with the strongest relationship (A, then E, then I…) to any already-selected department; ties broken by higher TCR.
  3. Placement: for every empty cell touching the layout, PR = Σ v(rel) × 1 (edge) or × 0.5 (corner). Place at the highest PR (ties: closest to the layout centroid).

In practice mode, pick any candidate — the log tells you how far you were from the algorithm's choice. A gap of 0 means you played perfect CORELAP.

Theory: the ALDEP procedure
  1. Select the first department at random.
  2. Scan for an unplaced department whose relationship with the last entered one meets the minimum level (strongest qualifying wins here). If none, pick at random.
  3. Fill the building in a vertical serpentine sweep of the chosen width, giving each department its area in consecutive cells.
  4. Score adjacency: A=64 · E=16 · I=4 · O=1 · U=0 · X=−1024 per edge-sharing pair. Run many times, keep the best.

Theory: the CRAFT procedure
  1. Start from any complete layout and a quantitative flow matrix.
  2. Cost: TC = Σᵢⱼ fᵢⱼ · dᵢⱼ with rectilinear centroid distances.
  3. Each iteration, estimate the cost change of every pairwise exchange, apply the best improving swap, and repeat until none helps — a local optimum. Multi-start shows how different beginnings end at different optima.

Simulated Annealing — climbing out of local optima

CRAFT stops at the first layout no single swap can improve. Simulated annealing (Kirkpatrick, Gelatt & Vecchi, 1983) fixes that: it sometimes accepts a worse layout, with probability P = e^(−Δ/T), so it can escape a local optimum. As the "temperature" T cools, those uphill moves fade out and the search settles. Same objective and flows as CRAFT — now solved as a Quadratic Assignment Problem over fixed locations.

Theory: why accept worse solutions?
  1. Propose a random neighbour (swap two departments between their locations). Compute Δ = new cost − old cost.
  2. If Δ < 0 (better) accept it. If Δ > 0 (worse) accept it anyway with probability e^(−Δ/T).
  3. High T early → many uphill moves → broad exploration. Low T late → almost only downhill → fine tuning.
  4. Cool geometrically: T ← α·T. Always remember the best layout ever seen, since the current one may be worse.

Genetic Algorithm — evolving a layout

A population-based metaheuristic (Holland, 1975; applied to layout from the late 1980s). Each "chromosome" is a whole layout; good layouts are combined by crossover and perturbed by mutation, generation after generation. It searches many regions at once, complementing single-point methods like CRAFT and SA. Same QAP objective and flows.

Theory: the evolutionary loop
  1. Encoding. A chromosome is a permutation assigning each department to a location.
  2. Selection. Tournament: pick k random layouts, keep the cheapest — better layouts breed more.
  3. Crossover (Order Crossover, OX). Copy a slice of one parent, fill the rest from the other in order, preserving a valid permutation.
  4. Mutation. Occasionally swap two departments — the source of new variation.
  5. Elitism. Carry the very best layouts unchanged so quality never regresses.

The gap between the best and average curves is the population's diversity; when it collapses, evolution has converged.

The methods landscape — where these fit

Every method here is, at heart, attacking the Quadratic Assignment Problem (Koopmans & Beckmann, 1957): assign facilities to locations to minimise Σ fᵢⱼ·dᵢⱼ. QAP is NP-hard, so exact optimisation is only feasible for small instances; everything else is a heuristic or metaheuristic trade-off between speed and quality.

Discussion prompts

Questions to explore
  • CORELAP is deterministic — rerun it and nothing changes. Is that a strength or a weakness compared to ALDEP?
  • Change one U to an X in the REL chart. Which algorithm reacts most dramatically, and why is the ALDEP X-penalty −1024?
  • Run CRAFT from five random starts, then run Simulated Annealing once. Does SA reliably match the best of the five? Why is that the whole point of accepting worse moves?
  • In the Genetic Algorithm, set mutation rate to 0. What happens to the best-vs-average gap after a few generations, and why does evolution stall without mutation?
  • Raise the department count to 8 and read the "search space (n!)" figure in the SA tab. How many layouts would exhaustive search test, and roughly how long at a million per second?
  • The REL→flow conversion (A=100 … U=0) is arbitrary. Propose a different mapping and see whether it changes which method wins.
